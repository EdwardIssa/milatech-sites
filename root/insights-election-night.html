Election Night in Michigan: What a Live Poll Can and Can’t Tell You

For the 2026 midterms, MILA ran a public election board for Michigan’s statewide races — open polls anyone could vote in, no account required. Election night was a genuine education in how easily three completely different numbers get mistaken for each other. This is what we learned, including the parts that make our own product look small. Especially those parts.

Three numbers that all look like “the score”

Start with the U.S. Senate race. In the early hours of November 4, the Associated Press called Michigan for Abdul El-Sayed. At the moment of the call, with roughly 92 percent of the expected vote reported, the count stood at about 52 percent for El-Sayed to 46 percent for Mike Rogers — reported figures, still being counted, not certified results. That is the first kind of number: vote share. It is a count of actual ballots, and it is the only one of the three that decides anything.

The second number came from the prediction markets. In the final stretch, market pricing on that same race sat around 75–25 — and a great many people read that as a predicted margin, as if the market expected a 75-to-25 landslide. It does not mean that. A 75–25 market price is a probability: traders collectively priced roughly a 75 percent chance of an El-Sayed win, at any margin — one vote or one million. Vote share and probability can move together, but they are different species. Confusing them is how a coin-flip race with a slight favorite gets reported as a blowout-in-waiting.

The third number is the one we make: a poll split — the share of people in a specific poll who picked each side. A poll split is neither a count of ballots nor a probability of winning. It is a measurement of the people who happened to be in that poll, answering at that moment. Which raises the only question that matters about any poll: which people, and how many?

Why MILA hides a split until ten ballots

MILA does not display a percentage split on a poll until it has at least ten ballots. Two reasons. The first is herding: a visible 90–10 split in the first hour doesn’t just report opinion, it manufactures it — people pile onto the winning side, argue differently, or stay silent. The number stops measuring the room and starts directing it. The second is arithmetic honesty. A split computed from three ballots can only be 100–0, 67–33, or some equally theatrical fiction. Showing it with the same visual confidence as a split from three thousand ballots would be lying with typography. So below ten ballots, MILA shows the count and says plainly: splits appear at ten. No number is more honest than a fake one.

The honest limits of our own board

Here is the part most product blogs would leave out. Our election board was small. Several of its races closed with no ballots at all, and not one race reached the ten-ballot threshold where a split would display. The board measured, faithfully, the opinions of almost nobody — and we are telling you that on our own site, because it is the most important sentence in this article. An open internet poll measures the people who found it and cared enough to tap. That population is not Michigan. It is not a sample of Michigan, a slice of Michigan, or a hint about Michigan. It is a room — and on election night, our room was mostly empty, while the state’s actual voters were being counted by the AP. A quiet board is still useful data about one thing: how much distribution a young platform does and doesn’t have yet. It says nothing about an election, and any poll operator who tells you otherwise is selling you the room as if it were the state.

How to read any poll you see this week

Four checks, ten seconds each, that work on our polls and everyone else’s:

  • What kind of number is it? Ballots counted, a probability priced by money, or people answering a question? The label matters more than the digits.
  • How many observations? A percentage without a count is a rumor in a suit. If the n is hidden, assume it’s small.
  • Who was asked? Registered voters, likely voters, a website’s visitors, a platform’s users — each is a different universe with a different answer.
  • When, and what changed since? A poll is a photograph, not a prophecy. A week-old split in a fast-moving race is history, not news.

Professional pollsters earn their keep on the second and third questions — sampling, likely-voter screens, weighting. Open platforms like ours earn ours on the first and fourth: labeling the number honestly and timestamping it. The failures happen in the gap, where a sentiment reading gets dressed up as a forecast and marched onto a chyron.

What a live poll is actually for

None of this makes open polling useless — it makes it specific. A live poll is a fast, honest read on a community’s mood and intensity: what people argue about, which questions split a room down the middle, how sentiment moves in the hour after news breaks. Used that way — as a thermometer for a room, clearly labeled with the size of the room — it is a real instrument. Used as a crystal ball, it’s a toy wearing a lab coat. We build MILA to be the instrument: real counts only, splits that refuse to appear before they mean anything, and results we’ll describe as plainly when they’re tiny as when they’re huge. Michigan’s voters decided Michigan’s races. Our job is the smaller, still-useful one: showing you what the people in the room actually said — and never pretending the room was the state.
